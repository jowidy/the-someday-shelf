Chapter 1 The longer deadline

When more time
buys less.

Mara misses reading. At work on Friday, she pictured an hour on the sofa with her novel. By the time she gets home, a few emails need answers, there’s dinner to make, and Saturday seems like a better bet. It did last Friday, too.

Researchers Suzanne Shu and Ayelet Gneezy wondered what happens when people can keep saying “another day” to something they actually want. In one experiment, they gave 64 students a $6 pastry voucher for a French café. Half had three weeks to use it. The others had two months.

Chapter 2 The cost of starting

What makes tonight
feel wrong?

Mara could read for half an hour. She’s hoping for two. Across the room, her housemate Ellis eyes his guitar. He remembers how good playing felt and how long it took to get past the rusty first minutes. Their hesitation looks similar from the outside.

“I’m saving it for a better night.”

The friend who recommended the novel loved it. Mara wants to give it her full attention. A good bottle of wine can sit unopened for years because no dinner feels quite special enough. Researchers call this occasion matching. Mara has started treating the novel the same way.

In a separate experiment, 501 adults imagined taking up a favorite activity again. Those considering a longer absence said they wanted a more special return and preferred to wait.

“I can’t face getting started.”

Ellis has a recording of a half-finished song. Listening to it means finding the session, tuning the guitar and hearing the passage he couldn’t get right last time. Scrolling asks less of him right now, even though he’d rather spend the evening playing.

Wouter Kool and his colleagues gave people a choice between laboratory tasks. People often picked the one that asked them to switch rules less often. For Ellis, the interesting question is how much of the effort comes before he plays a note. Would he reach for the guitar after dinner if it were already in tune?

Study notes · Kool and colleagues, 2010 ↗
“It’s another thing on my list.”

Mara kept a reading list so she could choose a book when the mood struck. Now she wants the newest book on it and feels obliged to read the oldest. If someone told her what she had to read, she might want to pick something else just to keep the choice hers. Psychologists call that impulse reactance. Whether a list you wrote yourself can create the same pressure is an open question.

A coffee break offers a clue about how leisure starts to feel like work. In one campus study, students who came for coffee and a cookie enjoyed the break more when they could drop in during a two-hour window than when they had chosen an exact time.

Chapter 3 The next clip

The ease of
switching away.

On the train home, Mara had opened her phone for the departure board. Ten stops later she was skipping between videos, still looking for something worth watching. It’s easy to imagine each new choice making the ride more interesting. Researchers tested that intuition.

Katy Tam and Michael Inzlicht had 159 people try two ways of watching videos. When viewers could switch between clips, they reported more boredom on average than when they stayed with one. The researchers saw less consistent results when people read articles and when they recruited beyond student groups.

Study notes · Tam & Inzlicht, 2024 ↗

Would a phone-free pause help?

Mara might put her phone away for the last few stops. Would it be easier to settle into her book when she got home? Tam and Inzlicht measured boredom while people watched clips. Whether a pause helps someone start a different activity afterward is a question their experiment leaves open.

Chapter 4 Picking it back up

What a return
has to carry.

Ellis used to play music every week with a friend. After a year apart, asking him over for half an hour feels almost inadequate. He keeps meaning to arrange a proper evening. That wish has already cost them several ordinary ones.

Linda Hagen and Ed O’Brien asked 1,000 people to identify two friends they felt similarly close to, one recently contacted and one they hadn’t spoken to in a while. Given the choice between texting the assigned friend and doing a longer, dull task alone, 63% chose the text for the recent friend. For the long-absent friend, 46% did.

Study notes · Hagen & O’Brien, 2025 ↗

Back at the table, Mara opens the novel while dishes dry in the rack. In three experiments, people enjoyed a break about as much with work still ahead as they did after finishing it. Some nights exhaustion settles the question. On others the first page can tell you more than another evening spent guessing.
