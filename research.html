THE RESEARCH

Why we postpone what we enjoy

The research on postponed pleasure, interrupted attention, and returning to activities that matter to us.

The short version

Why would someone put off an evening with a friend they miss? One possibility is that, after months apart, a quick drink feels too small for the occasion. Experiments on friendship, leisure and everyday purchases show several ways that wanting an experience can coexist with putting it off.

Research on gift certificates and returning to friends offers direct evidence of delay. Studies of mental effort, saving possessions, and persuasive messages offer related ideas, but do not establish why any particular person avoids a hobby.

Enjoyable things can be postponed

In a study of 64 university students, Suzanne Shu and Ayelet Gneezy gave participants a $6 French café gift certificate. Ten of 32 with three weeks to redeem it did so, compared with two of 32 given two months. A separate group predicted that the longer deadline would lead to more redemption.

The authors suggest people underestimate how busy they will be later. This small study concerns one café and one population, and only 33 of the original 64 answered the follow-up survey. It cannot tell us how often people delay hobbies or why a particular certificate went unused.

Source: Shu, S. B., & Gneezy, A. (2010). “Procrastination of Enjoyable Experiences.” Journal of Marketing Research, 47(5), 933–944. doi:10.1509/jmkr.47.5.933.

Waiting for the right occasion

Suzanne Shu and Marissa Sharif studied “occasion matching,” the tendency to reserve a special indulgence for a suitably special moment. In one choice study, a valuable, limited-use pass encouraged participants to wait and, in some conditions, choose a lower-ranked option. The effect depended on the experimental conditions, including anticipated regret.

A reader who saves a novel for an uninterrupted weekend may recognize the feeling. The pass experiment shows how people chose an occasion for a limited opportunity. Applying that finding to reading is an analogy.

Source: Shu, S. B., & Sharif, M. A. (2018). “Occasion Matching of Indulgences.” Journal of Marketing Behavior, 3(3), 211–239. doi:10.1561/107.00000052.

When saving makes something feel special

Jacqueline Rifkin and Jonah Berger report six studies in which forgoing use of possessions could make them feel more special, particularly when people attributed the decision to waiting for a later occasion. Greater perceived specialness could then restrict intended use to a narrower set of occasions.

Those studies concern possessions and usage intentions. A similar loop might arise with a neglected pastime, but that extension is an analogy.

Source: Rifkin, J. R., & Berger, J. (2021). “How Nonconsumption Can Turn Ordinary Items into Perceived Treasures.” Journal of the Association for Consumer Research, 6(3), 350–361. doi:10.1086/714363.

When a longer absence makes a return harder

Linda Hagen and Ed O’Brien studied what happens when people return to experiences they still want. In one experiment, 1,000 online participants identified both a recently contacted friend and a long-uncontacted friend, under instructions to choose friends they felt equally close to and comfortable contacting. Participants were assigned one friend and chose between sending a fun hello and doing a longer, boring solo task. Sixty-three percent chose the text in the short-gap condition, compared with 46% in the long-gap condition.

In another experiment, 501 adults imagined returning to a personally loved activity. The long-gap group expressed a stronger preference to delay and a stronger desire for an exceptionally valuable return. A separate study found that reflecting on how any occasion could feel special reduced stated preference for delay from 64% in the control group to 51%. These are controlled experiments, including imagined choices; they do not show that every long absence causes avoidance or that a reflection exercise will work for everyone.

Source: Hagen, L., & O’Brien, E. (2025). “Lost Time Undermines Return Behavior.” PNAS Nexus, 4(6), pgaf156. doi:10.1093/pnasnexus/pgaf156.

The mental effort of getting started

Across laboratory tasks, Wouter Kool and colleagues found that people often chose options requiring less cognitive control, including fewer switches between tasks. This supports the idea that anticipated mental demand can influence choices.

Reading a complex novel, setting up a game, or picking up an instrument may feel effortful at first. That application is plausible but was not measured in these laboratory tasks. Fatigue and competing demands may also change what feels possible. Effort can itself be rewarding, and choosing an easier activity does not necessarily signal a loss of interest.

Source: Kool, W., McGuire, J. T., Rosen, Z. B., & Botvinick, M. M. (2010). “Decision Making and the Avoidance of Cognitive Demand.” Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 139(4), 665–682. doi:10.1037/a0020198.

Scheduling and the quality of leisure

Gabriela Tonietto and Selin Malkoc examined scheduled leisure across 13 studies. Precisely scheduled activities sometimes felt more work-like and less enjoyable than impromptu or roughly scheduled activities. In a campus experiment, 148 students received tickets for coffee and a cookie. Among the 54 who came, those offered a two-hour window enjoyed the break more than those who had picked a specific time. The researchers could only ask about enjoyment among people who showed up.

Scheduling may also help an activity happen at all. Try a loose window, such as “sometime this afternoon,” if a fixed appointment makes leisure feel rigid. If a calendar helps you protect the time, use it.

Source: Tonietto, G. N., & Malkoc, S. A. (2016). “The Calendar Mindset: Scheduling Takes the Fun Out and Puts the Work In.” Journal of Marketing Research, 53(6), 922–936. doi:10.1509/jmr.14.0591.

When a list starts to feel like pressure

Being told “you must” can make someone want to reclaim the choice. Psychologists call that response reactance. A 2025 review of 35 studies examined how people responded to health messages that used controlling language. Whether a person feels the same pushback toward their own reading list remains an open question.

One way to explore that distinction is to change what the list asks of you. A shelf of books to browse leaves you free to skip a title. Number those same books and skipping the first one can feel like breaking a rule.

More switching is not always more refreshing

In seven experiments with 1,223 participants, Katy Tam and Michael Inzlicht examined switching between and within digital content. In one study, 159 people experienced both watching one video and being able to switch among videos. Average boredom was 3.26 without switching and 3.72 with switching on the study scale. The result varied with the audience and material. Experiments with articles and nonstudent samples produced less consistent results.

Source: Tam, K. Y. Y., & Inzlicht, M. (2024). “Fast-Forward to Boredom: How Switching Behavior on Digital Media Makes People More Bored.” Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 153(10), 2409–2426. doi:10.1037/xge0001639.

Permission to enjoy something now

Ran Kivetz and Anat Keinan use hyperopia to describe a habit of passing up pleasures today for the sake of the future. Their research suggests that, from a longer time perspective, people may increasingly regret missing pleasurable opportunities. It does not mean work is unimportant or that every act of restraint will be regretted.

Source: Kivetz, R., & Keinan, A. (2006). “Repenting Hyperopia: An Analysis of Self-Control Regrets.” Journal of Consumer Research, 33(2), 273–282. doi:10.1086/506308.

A small plan, held lightly

“If I make tea after dinner, I’ll draw one object in my sketchbook.” Psychologists call this kind of if-then plan an implementation intention. Making the tea can serve as a signal to begin. Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran’s review synthesized 94 tests from 63 reports and found that people who made plans like these were more likely, on average, to follow through on their goals. The review covered many kinds of goals. Which signals work best for returning to a hobby remains open.

Source: Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). “Implementation Intentions and Goal Achievement: A Meta-Analysis of Effects and Processes.” Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38, 69–119. doi:10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1.

Different minds, different circumstances

Postponing a hobby tells you little on its own about why someone struggles to begin. One reader may have lost interest. Another may still love the book and have half an hour free only after a full day of caregiving.

Do people with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) postpone enjoyable experiences more often? What about people who are especially sensitive to sensory input, a trait studied by Elaine and Arthur Aron? The leisure studies discussed here give us no basis for ranking groups by how often this happens.

Other useful threads

Leisure before work. Across four studies, O’Brien and Roney found that people anticipated unfinished work would spoil leisure. In three experiments involving a game, massage, snacks, and videos, reported enjoyment was similar before and after work. This does not mean looming obligations never disrupt enjoyment. O’Brien, E., & Roney, E. (2017). “Worth the Wait? Leisure Can Be Just as Enjoyable With Work Left Undone.” Psychological Science, 28(7), 1000–1015. doi:10.1177/0956797617701749.

Returning to the familiar. In studies of experiences such as a museum visit, a movie, and a video game, people predicted that repeating the experience would be less enjoyable than other participants reported it actually was. This is a reason to remain curious about revisiting something, not a promise every repeat will be satisfying. O’Brien, E. (2019). “Enjoy It Again: Repeat Experiences Are Less Repetitive Than People Think.” Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 116(4), 519–540. doi:10.1037/pspa0000147.

Stories and attention. Green and Brock (2000) investigated narrative transportation, or absorption in a story, and its relationship to beliefs. Zwaan and Radvansky (1998) reviewed how readers build mental representations of situations described in language. These papers ask how readers get absorbed in a story and make sense of it. How much attention someone needs to enjoy a chapter on a tired evening is a different question. Green, M. C., & Brock, T. C., Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 79(5), 701–721; Zwaan, R. A., & Radvansky, G. A., Psychological Bulletin, 123(2), 162–185, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.123.2.162.

Leisure and autonomy. Pestana, Codina, and Valenzuela examined seven-day time records and procrastination questionnaires in 185 university students. They looked for patterns between how students spent their free time and how often they said they put things off. Those observations cannot establish which caused which. Pestana, J. V., Codina, N., & Valenzuela, R. (2020). “Leisure and Procrastination, a Quest for Autonomy in Free Time Investments: Task Avoidance or Accomplishment?” Frontiers in Psychology, 10, 2918. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02918.

Boredom. Bench and Lench propose that boredom can motivate a change of activity. This is a proposed explanation for why someone might seek a new activity when the current one stops holding their attention. Whether sitting with boredom makes a particular hobby more appealing would need its own test. Bench, S. W., & Lench, H. C. (2013). “On the Function of Boredom.” Behavioral Sciences, 3(3), 459–472. doi:10.3390/bs3030459.

Further reading on habits and choice. For more on attention, see Therriault and colleagues (2015) on working memory and leisure and Schnauber-Stockmann and colleagues (2018) on how habits shape media choices. For more on choice, see Shu (2008) on passing up an option in hopes of a better one later and Rosenberg and Siegel (2018) on reactance.
